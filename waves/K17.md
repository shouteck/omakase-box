# K17: the demo tells the product's story, and leaves nothing behind

Milestone: K (kitchen) · Station: evidence · Base: master at dispatch, after BT11c · Branch: `wave/K17` · Timebox: 120 minutes; at expiry the cook stops and reports where it is, whatever state that is

**Pre-brief.** Written by the CTO on 14 October 2026 after the owner watched K16's video and the CTO read the whole real-run transcript (`kitchen/demo/bt11b-real-transcript.txt`), four of its stills and the owner's settings file. A kitchen wave: it cooks only when named. Each item below is something seen in that run, not a guess.

## What is wrong today

**It changes the owner's real settings and does not put them back.** In real mode the replay runs `config set reward-limit 50`, `config set reward 5` and `allow` against the owner's own settings file. After K16 that file says every bounty, on every repository, states a $5 reward with a $50 limit. The owner did not set that. A bounty approved on the owner's working repository would now promise $5 in public.

**It tells the story wrongly.**

- Step 3 is captioned "the posting agent sets the reward ($5) and creates the bounty". The reward is the owner's setting and an agent is never asked about money. The same step also runs the owner's `allow`, the most important moment in the product, without a word.
- Nothing says that `create` is a private draft and `post` is the public act, so two steps look like one too many.
- The reward is $5 and the test payment link is for $1, while the tool's own message says "check the checkout shows $5".
- Both solvers are the owner's GitHub account. The output says "pull request 23 by shouteck", "pay $5 to shouteck", "check the account name belongs to shouteck": the demo shows the owner paying themselves and never says why.
- Its first line reads "(BT11b), a walkthrough": a kitchen wave's name in something meant for strangers.

**It is hard to read.**

- Every tool command is shown as `C:\Program Files\nodejs\node.exe C:\Users\…\cli.js bounty …` and wraps over two lines. A viewer should see `box bounty …`.
- Steps 5 and 6 print the whole reply from creating a pull request, about 16,000 characters of JSON each.
- Git's "remote:" lines, line-ending warnings and the owner's home-folder paths fill the screen; step 2 is eight lines of repository plumbing.
- The pace does not follow the amount of text: step 3 prints about thirty lines and is on screen for four seconds.
- The still for each step is taken as the step begins, so `step-03.png` shows steps 1 and 2 and the index points a reviewer at the wrong picture.

**It leaves things on GitHub.** After seven real runs the sandbox has fourteen `solver-a-…` and `solver-b-…` branches. The closing lines say an issue "will be closed" after it has been.

## Outcome

A person who has never seen the tool watches one short video and can say: who the owner is and the one thing they decided; that an agent drafted a bounty privately and then posted it; that two strangers answered; that the agent read both, merged one, and the tool told it whom the owner should pay. Running the demo changes nothing of the owner's and leaves the sandbox as it found it, apart from closed test issues and pull requests it names.

**The owner's settings are never touched**

- In every mode the replay points the tool at a settings folder of its own in its scratch area, holding only what the demo needs: the sandbox allowed, the demo's reward and limit. The owner's real settings file is byte-for-byte the same before and after a run, and a test proves it on the stand-in. The real sign-in to GitHub is still the machine's own.
- **The owner's file is not repaired by this wave.** Its reward and limit were written by K16's runs; putting them back is the owner's own command, and the head chef has told the owner which. The Result records what the file said at dispatch.

**The story, in the order a stranger needs it**

- A step of its own, before any agent acts: **the owner, once**. After BT11c that is one command: the owner allows this repository and states its reward and limit together. The tool's own words about what allowing means stay on screen long enough to read.
- The agent's steps are captioned as what an agent does: drafts the bounty, privately; posts it, publicly. The caption for the draft says nothing has left the machine, which the tool's own output confirms on the next line.
- The reward is $1, the amount the owner's test payment link asks for.
- The title card says in plain words that both solvers are played by the owner's own GitHub account because this is a test, that in real use they are other people, and that this is why the pay message names the owner's account.
- No wave name, no kitchen word, in anything a viewer sees. The title says what the product does.
- Captions are short, one line, and say who is acting first.

**Readable**

- A tool command is shown as `box bounty …`. What is run is unchanged; only what is displayed is shortened, and the transcript keeps the full command beside it.
- Paths under the owner's home folder and the scratch area are shown short (for example `<project>` and `<settings>`), in the video and in the committed transcript.
- Output that is not the product's is cut to what matters: creating a pull request shows its address, not the reply; Git's "remote:" lines and line-ending warnings are not shown; setting up the example repository is one caption and at most two lines.
- **Nothing the product prints is reworded, reordered or dropped.** Where the tool's own output is long, the step simply stays on screen longer.
- The pause after a step follows how much text it showed, within stated limits; the Result gives the length of the video and of the longest step.
- The still for a step is taken when the step has finished printing, and the index names the picture that shows it.

**Clean**

- At the end of a run, and on Ctrl+C, the replay closes what it opened and deletes the solver branches it created. The closing lines are true at the moment they are printed.
- The fourteen solver branches left by earlier runs are deleted once, by exact name, after confirming each belongs to a closed pull request of a closed demo bounty. The two `fix/…` branches from K15 are not this wave's and are left.
- A test on the stand-in checks that a run ends with no branch of its own left.
