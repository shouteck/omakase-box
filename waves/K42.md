# K42: the platform, designed from what already works

Milestone: PF (the platform) · Station: evidence · Base: `master at dispatch` · Branch: `wave/K42` · Timebox: 240 minutes; at expiry, report partial findings and stop
Owner decisions: D-2026-10-20-platform

**Brief.** Written by the CTO on 20 October 2026. This is the first wave of the platform milestone. **It is research and design only: no product code, no accounts, no sign-ups, no money.** It ends with a design document and a short list of decisions for the owner, each with one recommendation. Build waves are briefed from it. Cook: high; reviewer: high, because the design binds every later wave and handles other people's money.

## Why

On 20 October 2026 the owner decided that box bounty becomes a platform: a place where bounties can be seen beyond GitHub issues, and where payment is made end to end. Until now, the product rules excluded "a marketplace or site of our own" and "holding anyone's money" (PLAN.md, "Not in this plan"). Payment was the owner opening a solver's Stripe link by hand. The owner judged that a hindrance to the whole loop and decided the product becomes a platform. Agents paying people already works only through platforms in the middle, which sign up the people being paid and move the money: Mechanical Turk, Upwork, bounty sites, RentAHuman. Monid does the same for businesses. The owner named two jobs for the platform:

1. **Bounties can be seen**, not only as GitHub issues.
2. **Payment is made end to end**, so a merged solution gets its solver paid without the owner clicking a link.

## Outcome

`docs/box-v4/PLATFORM.md`, a design the owner can approve, grounded in what successful and failed platforms did, with:

- every money and identity duty named and assigned (to Stripe or a similar provider, to the platform, to the owner, or to the solver);
- the smallest first version that completes the loop;
- the build waves, in order, each small enough to review once;
- the owner's decisions, each with one recommendation and its cost.

For each: what made solvers trust they would be paid, what made posters trust the work, and what went wrong. Facts only, each with its source.

### 2. How the money moves

- **The main candidate is Stripe Connect.** From Stripe's own documentation, answer:
  - **Account configuration** for solvers: who does identity checks, onboarding effort, the dashboard solvers get.
  - **Charge type:** destination charges, or separate charges and transfers.
  - **Funding at posting versus charging at merge.** How long Stripe lets funds wait before transfer, and what that means for a bounty open for weeks. Refund on close, and its fees.
  - **Countries:** where individuals can be paid out; currencies.
  - **Tax:** what the platform must report for paid solvers (for example US 1099 forms), and what Stripe does for it.
  - **Fees:** Stripe's fees and who bears them; whether the platform takes a fee.
  - **Disputes and chargebacks** on the poster's card after a solver was paid: who is liable.
  - **Business requirements** for the platform account: legal entity, country, prohibited categories, what Stripe reviews.
- **Alternatives, compared on the same points:** PayPal (marketplace and payouts), Wise Platform, and stablecoins (K32's x402 design) as an optional second rail.
- **The "never holds money" question:** state exactly who holds funds at each moment under the recommended design, and which licences or duties fall on the platform versus the provider. Where the answer needs a lawyer, say so plainly. Never guess.

### 3. What the platform is

1. **The bounty board.** A public page listing open bounties with a page per bounty: task, repository, reward, funded or not, state and solutions. What it reads (the issue's machine-readable `box-bounty-format` block, the Git-ref memory, its own records) and what stays only on GitHub.
2. **Accounts:**
   - **Owner:** signs in with GitHub, connects the repositories they allow, funds rewards, sets limits.
   - **Solver:** signs in with GitHub and onboards for payouts once.
   - **Agent:** acts for an owner through a token scoped by the owner's limits (the autonomy-with-guardrails rule).
   - Keep the owner's surface as small as the CLI's: allow, deny, config.
3. **The loop end to end:**
   - **Post:** `create` and `post` on the CLI, then the bounty appears on the board, funded or not.
   - **Solve:** pull request, and the solver's claim names their platform account instead of a Stripe link.
   - **Decide:** `apply` merges.
   - **Pay:** the platform **verifies the merge on GitHub itself** (never trusts the CLI's word), then pays the solver within the owner's limits, with "ask me" as the default.
   - **Record:** payment proof on the record, in the memory and on the issue.
   - **Close without a merge:** the refund path.
4. **How the CLI talks to the platform:** authentication (device flow or token), what is sent and when, offline behaviour, and what happens when the platform is down. Nothing in the loop should silently fail.
5. **The three hard stops on a platform:** never-ship files, private repositories, nothing beyond the owner's authority. Show how each still holds when a server is involved; the board must never show anything from a private repository. Also: the platform still runs nothing a solver wrote, and still does not judge solutions.
6. **Abuse:** fake claims, solvers claiming others' work, posters refusing to pay after merging (the platform's merge check answers this), money laundering through self-dealing, and the RentAHuman abuse classes.

### 4. Running it, at zero cost

**Owner's constraint (20 October 2026):** use widely accepted tools, Vercel named, and **spend no money**. That means free tiers only:

- no paid plan;
- no purchased domain: a free subdomain such as `*.vercel.app` until the owner decides otherwise;
- no paid add-on.

Rules for this section:

- **Stack:** Vercel with Next.js for the site and its server functions. GitHub OAuth for sign-in. One managed database with a free tier (compare at least two, for example Neon Postgres and Supabase). Stripe Connect for money.
- **Read each free tier's terms, not just its limits.** Vercel's free Hobby plan is described as for personal, non-commercial use, and a platform moving money for others may count as commercial. Find Vercel's exact current wording. If the platform does not fit, name the closest free option that does (for example Cloudflare Pages and Workers, or Netlify's free tier) and say what changes. Do the same for the database's terms.
- **Free-tier limits** at zero, 100 and 10,000 bounties: requests, function time, database size and connections, bandwidth. Name the first limit we would hit, and roughly when.
- **Money we never pay versus money the platform moves.** Stripe has no monthly fee for a basic account, but takes fees on each payment and possibly on each payout or active connected account. State every fee, and **who bears it** under the recommended design (poster, solver or platform). Any fee that would fall on us is a cost and goes to the owner as a decision.
- **What the owner must set up:** Vercel account, database account, GitHub OAuth app, Stripe platform account (business details, which may require a legal entity), terms of service, privacy policy. Count them step by step, all at $0, or flagged where $0 is impossible.
- **What changes in the CLI and its docs**, and what becomes legacy: the `pay-to:` Stripe link and `config set paid`.
