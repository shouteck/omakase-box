# K33: the kitchen reviewed on its own record: what it costs, what pays, what should change

Milestone: K (kitchen) · Station: evidence · Base: master at dispatch · Branch: `wave/K33` · Timebox: 240 minutes; at expiry the cook stops and reports what it has

**Brief.** Written by the CTO on 18 October 2026 at the owner's request: "a wave where they scrutinise our kitchen structure to come up with critiques or suggestions on how we run our day-to-day kitchen." An investigation: **it changes no product code, no test and no kitchen document.** It measures how the kitchen has actually run, reads its rules as a newcomer would, compares them with established practice, and proposes at most ten ranked changes. **Cook it with a fresh agent that did not write the rulebook**, and the pass likewise; the head chef does not cook or review it. Detailed for a cook without deep reasoning.

## Why

The kitchen has run about sixty waves since 5 October 2026 across two hosts (Devin and Claude Code) and several models, under rules that grew one lesson at a time: AGENTS.md, `docs/box-v4/KITCHEN.md`, five role files in `kitchen/roles/`, four station cards in `kitchen/stations/`, `templates/wave.md`, `START.md`, `docs/box-v4/OVERNIGHT.md`, and the scripts that enforce them (`scripts/verify.mjs`, `suite.mjs`, `kitchen-stats.mjs`). Each rule was added for a reason; nobody has reviewed the whole. The head chef has seen friction it cannot judge from inside, for example:
- K22 stalled for hours rerunning the full suite on a commit that changed only a wave file (fixed by K23 and K24);
- kitchen-document edits broke the hosted check twice (the 20 KB rulebook limit; doc-only pushes skip the check);
- the owner rejected and the head chef rewrote several briefs before dispatch (BT32 three times);
- the cook usually works in the main checkout, so the head chef commits its own documents through temporary worktrees;
- lower-reasoning cooks often needed a correction round, which the passes caught.

These are leads, not conclusions.

## Outcome

`kitchen/reviews/K33-kitchen.md`: the kitchen's measured record, a newcomer's reading of its rules, a comparison with established practice for teams of coding agents, and at most ten changes ranked by measured cost, each with what it would remove or add, who decides it, and how its effect would be measured afterwards.

## Method: five steps, with time budgets

**Step 1: the record, measured (70 minutes).** Write a small script in `kitchen/review-k33/` that reads the wave files, the run summaries and the Git log, and prints, per wave since 5 October 2026 (product `BT*`, kitchen `K*`, corrections `*-ci`):
- elapsed time from the brief's first commit to its merge commit, and the merge-to-close time;
- the number of verify runs on the wave's commits and their total time; how many failed, and of those how many only on a timeout;
- correction rounds (a pass FAIL followed by more cook commits) and what each FAIL found (from the Result);
- whether the hosted run after the merge was red, and why (from the Result or the run log);
- whether the brief was rewritten after the owner saw it, and how many times;
- the host that cooked it (Devin or Claude Code, from the Result's Worker and Reviewer lines), where stated.
A missing value is printed as missing, never as zero (the evidence card's rule). Then summarise: the median and slowest waves by kind; where the time went (cooking, verify, review, correction, waiting for the owner); FAIL rate by host and by brief style (the detailed briefs since 15 October versus earlier ones); red hosted runs and their causes; the ten most expensive single events, each with its wave.

**Step 2: what the reviews caught (30 minutes).** From every pass's notes: the defects each pass found that the cook had missed, classed as security, correctness, an untrue claim in the Result, or scope. Count them per host and brief style. This is the evidence for what the two-agent structure pays for.

**Step 3: the rulebook, read as a newcomer (50 minutes).** Read every rule file as a fresh cook would before a wave, and record:
- how much a cook must read (count the lines and characters of the required reading AGENTS.md names, and of the role, station card and wave file);
- contradictions between files (quote both);
- rules that are stale (name something that no longer exists, for example the older Box platform K29 is removing) or that no wave in step 1 ever exercised;
- rules that exist in a document but are not enforced by a script, and rules enforced by a script but not stated anywhere a cook reads;
- where the same rule is written in several places;
- the steps of a wave from `START.md` to close, as the head chef actually performs them (from the wave files and Git log), against what KITCHEN.md says.

**Step 4: established practice (40 minutes).** From current public sources (primary where possible: the documentation of the tools and frameworks themselves, and engineering write-ups by the teams that run them), how others run teams of coding agents on one codebase: how work is split and described (briefs, specs, tickets); one writer per branch and the use of worktrees; independent review and how it is kept independent; test strategy and CI for agents; how humans stay in the loop; how lessons are kept. Compare with pstack (`docs/box-v4/PSTACK.md`, the skill files) and our rulebook. Cite every claim; label vendor material as such.

**Step 5: the proposals (50 minutes).** At most ten changes, ranked by the measured cost they remove (step 1) or the risk they cover (step 2). For each, one paragraph: the problem with its evidence (numbers, quoted lines); the change, exactly (which file, which rule, added or removed); what it would cost the kitchen; who decides (the head chef: rulebook, tooling and process; the owner: anything in AGENTS.md's "Who decides what", spending, hosts, models); and how its effect would be measured after one run. Include at least: what to **remove** from the rulebook (subtract before adding); whether cooks should always work in their own worktree; how wave files are kept from making a verify run dirty; how briefs could be checked against the owner's known decisions before the owner sees them. Then the owner's page.

## The report (`kitchen/reviews/K33-kitchen.md`)

1. **One page first, for the owner, in plain words:** how the kitchen has actually run (a few numbers); what it does well, with evidence; the three changes that would help most; and what needs the owner's decision.
2. Steps 1 to 5 in order, every number naming the records it came from, every outside claim cited.
3. What was not examined, and why.
