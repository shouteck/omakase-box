## The owner's thesis (the starting point, to be tested, not assumed)

1. **Free for everyone.** No platform fee for posters or solvers: traction matters more than revenue. (Since 21 October 2026 the platform carries no money at all, D-2026-10-21-no-platform-money: owners pay solvers directly, and the platform shows public payment facts.)
2. **The earlier bounty sites failed partly because people, not agents, created the bounties.** Demand was scarce and slow. Agents can create demand continuously, as part of their work.
3. **The real demand arrives with agents that run autonomously over long horizons.** An agent working for hours or days meets things it cannot or should not do, or things outside its context, and handing them off becomes the sensible move. This is a bet on timing.
4. **A healthy ecosystem used mainly by agents, on both sides.** Agents post; agents run by people solve; people set limits and watch.
5. **Supply and demand must both be solved.** The open question is how to make solvers want to solve our tasks.

## Why

- **History:** Bountysource failed; Polar removed issue funding; Algora now presents a hiring service; IssueHunt became a security bug-bounty site; Gitcoin handed off bounties; Mechanical Turk closed on 30 September 2026 (K42, `docs/box-v4/PLATFORM.md` section 1).
- **Where demand fell short:** Opire and RentAHuman both had more people willing to work than tasks, which supports the thesis that demand was the bottleneck. But RentAHuman also shows agent-posted demand can be flaky ("engage-then-ghost").
- **What we saw ourselves:** in K31, today's agents rightly declined to hand off small tasks.

The strategy must say how the thesis would win, and what would show it is wrong.

## Questions, in order

1. **The market, sized from sources.**
   - How many developers use coding agents.
   - How common long-running autonomous use is: background agents, agents running for hours, multi-agent setups. Which hosts offer it (for example Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Devin, Jules) and how they report its growth.
   - How many public repositories carry agent instruction files.
   - Facts with sources only; vendor numbers are labelled as claims.
2. **Why an agent hands work off.** Start from these candidate reasons and find evidence for or against each:
   - access it lacks: hardware, devices, paid services, credentials it must not use;
   - knowledge outside its context;
   - rules forbidding it, as in K31b;
   - parallelism: offloading a side task while it continues;
   - cost: cheaper elsewhere than its own budget;
   - repeated failure;
   - an independent check by someone else.

   **Experiment:**
   - Give two or three fresh agents a long, realistic task in a scratch repository, with the tool installed and described, each seeded with one obstacle from the list above.
   - Record whether and when each hands off, with its stated reason, and at what point it should have.
   - Compare with K31 and K31b.

   This experiment is the closest test of thesis point 3 available today. Run it locally, with no GitHub posts beyond the sandbox at $0 (D-2026-10-19-k31-sandbox-go) if a post is needed at all.
3. **Who posts first.** Rank the candidates by evidence:
   - people running long autonomous agents;
   - solo developers with agents;
   - small startups;
   - open-source maintainers;
   - agencies.

   What would each hand off, and through which agent host?
4. **Why solvers answer: the supply side.**
   - **Agents as solvers:** what exists today for a person's agent to find and do paid work (agent marketplaces, agent-to-agent protocols and registries, RentAHuman's agent side). Is "put your idle agent to work and earn" a pitch anyone already makes, and with what result? Search before claiming novelty.
   - **Solver economics:** what a solver agent's compute costs for a typical bounty (from public model pricing), against the reward needed to make it worth running. Find the reward level below which only hobbyists answer.
   - **Trust without money in the middle:** what made solvers trust and return on Upwork, Algora, Gitcoin and Opire. **Are public payment facts enough?** Our model is "the owner pays directly; this owner's record: N merged, N recorded paid, N confirmed received by the solver", plus solver track records. Where did platforms whose creators paid directly (Opire, early GitHub-issue bounties) succeed or fail? Test the model on fresh agents playing solvers: would they take a bounty from an owner with no record, or with a good one?
   - **Track record:** whether a public, facts-only history of a solver's merged and paid solutions (no ratings) changes behaviour on comparable platforms. The owner approved facts-only track records on 21 October 2026 (D-2026-10-21-platform-v1).
   - **The flood risk:** the documented backlash of maintainers against low-quality AI pull requests. How a flood would hit a poster whose agent must read every solution, and what triage would help (gap G4).
5. **Discovery built for machines first, at $0.**
   - How solver agents could find bounties: a feed, an API, an MCP server ("bounties matching my skills"), the `box-bounty-format` block, GitHub search.
   - Which free registries list MCP servers, skills and plugins for the major hosts, and what a listing requires.
   - How the tool already reaches posting agents (the instruction block, the hook).
6. **Channels for the people behind the agents, at $0:** Show HN, Product Hunt, X with the K41 film, Reddit's agent communities, Discord, dev.to. For each: the audience, a successful example, the venue's self-promotion rules, and the risks.
7. **Positioning.** Write three one-liners for each side: for an agent's owner ("let your agent hand off what it can't do"), and for a solver ("put your agent to work"). Test each on two fresh agents playing that user ("would you try this, and why not?"). Record the answers.
8. **Staying free, and sustaining it later.** How comparable free developer tools and marketplaces stayed free while growing, and what they charged once they did charge (with sources). This is a recommendation for later; pricing is the owner's.
9. **The cold start, in sequence.**
   - Which side to seed first, and how at $0. For example:
     - the kitchen's own projects posting real bounties;
     - friendly projects;
     - solver agents invited through the registries in question 5;
     - **the kitchen solving a few early bounties itself, clearly labelled** (the owner's decision: it can look like manufactured activity).
   - What would tell us to stop or change course.
   - Order and dependencies only, never dates.
10. **What we measure.** The plan's measure (of bounties posted, how many got a solution, and how long it took) plus at most five more. Include at least: the share of bounties posted by agents without a human prompt, and the share of solutions from agents. For each, how it is collected without surveillance beyond what the product records, and the threshold that would mean the bet is working, or not.
