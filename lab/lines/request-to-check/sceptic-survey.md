## 2. Framing attacks

### (a) "About one in five accepted solutions is wrong"

As a statement about developer-written tests in general: **does not survive.** As a statement about one study's estimate on one benchmark: survives with its qualifiers restored.

- One in five is one paper's number. Other estimates on the same benchmark family, by different methods: about 1% (UTBoost's tests on the same 11,041 patches, per SWE-ABS's table), 7.8% (fail the full developer suite, Wang), about 8.5% certainly incorrect by differential testing (Wang), 15.2% (SWE-ABS excluding its own overfitting instances), 19.7 to 19.8% (SWE-ABS headline), about 31% "suspicious" (SWE-Bench+, original SWE-bench, one weak 2024 agent). The honest statement is a range of roughly 1 in 12 to 1 in 5 on SWE-bench Verified, depending on how hard one looks, with the upper end resting on model-written tests anchored to the gold patch.
- The upper figure uses an instrument that the charter would not accept uncalibrated: an LLM-written test suite acting as judge, whose authors report a 10.6% instance-level false-reject rate before manual correction, and no manual check of the 2,184 rejections beyond a sample of 100.
- What is being measured is not "the developers' tests". SWE-bench runs a chosen subset of the tests from the fixing pull request. Wang's 7.8% exists precisely because the benchmark does not run the full developer suite. SWE-bench Verified was also filtered to remove tasks whose tests were too specific, which selects toward permissive tests.
- The rate is a property of the patch population as much as of the tests: patches from top agents tuned against this benchmark, on 12 Python libraries, with tests hidden. A recipient who sees the evaluator (this product) is a different population, and the direction of the difference is unknown.
- `related-work.md` states most of these limits in its "Read together" paragraph. `survey.md` point 1 and the line README drop them. The README's "accept roughly one wrong solution in five" is also the wrong way round as a rate: 19.7% is the share of accepted patches that were later rejected, not the share of wrong patches that get accepted. The line's own definition of false accept ("the share of wrong changes it accepts") is a different quantity, and no cited source measures it.

The METR sentence beside it has the comparator error described in the table.

### (b) "The literature suggests the cheapest trustworthy route for a new behaviour is a model-drafted failing check that the requester confirms"

**Does not survive, even at "suggests".** Three words are unearned.

- **"Cheapest":** no source compares the requester's cost across routes. The survey's own section 4 says the cost evidence is "thin" and that nothing compares specifying by a check with doing the change. A superlative cannot rest on an absent comparison.
- **"Trustworthy":** TiCoder measured whether 15 people picked correct code among five candidates on three textbook functions, not the error rate of a confirmed check. Only the pass/fail variant was significant; the variant where the user states the expected output was not. The large simulated gain assumes a user who answers from the reference implementation. The paper itself warns the result may not scale.
- **"For a new behaviour":** the 63% fail-to-pass rate is on SWE-bench-derived issues, mostly bug reports, scored against the known human fix. The survey's own sources say feature requests are where agents do worst (37.72%, 29.94%) and where implicit requirements bite. No cited source measures fail-to-pass test generation for new behaviour, and none measures requester confirmation at repository scale.
- Evidence in the survey that cuts against the route is not weighed against it: generated oracles drift toward current behaviour (Konstantinou), which is exactly the error a confirming requester must catch; and the one filter result (SWT-Bench) keeps 20% of correct fixes and still passes more wrong than right.
- Evidence the survey missed cuts against it further (section 3 below): people misjudge machine-generated specifications at high rates.

What survives: requester-confirmed generated tests are a plausible candidate route with one small positive human study at function scale. That is a hypothesis for the benchmark, not a finding of the literature. The README's baselines section already treats it that way; the "Current best answer" bullet does not.

Also in the README: "Models now write a usable failing test from a bug report about 63% of the time". The source measures fail-to-pass against the reference fix. `related-work.md` itself notes such a test "can still be too narrow or too weak". "Usable" is not earned.

### (c) Line 2: a zero-cost study can measure steps and tokens well and success only coarsely

**Half survives.**

- "Success only coarsely" survives and is understated. Khatri's own power analysis: with 17 tasks and 3 repeats even a 30 point effect is detected only 57% of the time; 10 points needs 120 to 200 tasks; more repeats barely help. The survey's sentence "288 runs only bounded it to 10 to 15 points" misreads this. The paper calls that bound descriptive and "not a powered equivalence claim". The limiter is the number of distinct tasks, not runs. The lab's formulation should count tasks.
- "Can probably measure steps and tokens well" is not supported by anything cited. It is the lab's inference and should be labelled so. The one cited study that measured them found tool calls, output tokens and duration flat or non-significant at this scale. Paired continuous measures do have more power than a binary outcome, and a Box changes how much source exists to explore, so a larger efficiency effect than a context file produces is plausible. Plausible is the earned word.
- The "prior from the field" that a success difference will be small rests on two studies of adding a context file. A Box removes source. The retrieval study points the other way but its outcome is file F1, not task success. The field supplies no prior for this manipulation in either direction. The survey says the question is open and then states a prior anyway.
- A confound the survey does not raise: fewer steps and tokens in a smaller package is close to mechanical (less to read) and does not show the Box helps. The formulation needs to say what efficiency result would count as evidence rather than arithmetic.

## 3. Prior work missed for Line 1

A few searches, not exhaustive.

1. **Whether people can judge machine-generated checks.** Staats, Hong, Kim, Rothermel, "Understanding User Understanding: Determining Correctness of Generated Program Invariants", ISSTA 2012. Search summary: users misclassified 9.1% to 31.7% of correct invariants and 26.1% to 58.6% of incorrect ones. This is direct evidence on the "requester confirms" step and it is unfavourable. It would change the survey's point 4, which presents requester confirmation as "the one route with human evidence" and cites only the favourable study. Not opened in full; treat the numbers as a lead.
2. **Whether generated tests help the humans who receive them.** Fraser, Staats, McMinn, Arcuri, Padberg, "Does Automated Unit Test Generation Really Help Software Testers? A Controlled Empirical Study", ACM TOSEM 24(4), 2015. Search summary: 49 subjects; large coverage gains, "no measurable improvement in the number of bugs actually found". Pre-LLM, but it is the controlled-experiment form of the Konstantinou finding and it has people in it.
3. **The human oracle cost literature.** The survey names the test oracle problem but cites none of its literature (the Barr, Harman, McMinn, Shahbaz, Yoo survey in IEEE TSE 2015 is the standard entry point; named from memory, not opened this session). That literature treats the cost of a human judging test outputs as a quantity to minimise, which is this line's "requester cost" under an older name.
4. **Requester-side acceptance tests before LLMs.** Ricca, Torchiano, Di Penta, Ceccato, Tonella, "Using acceptance tests as a support for clarifying requirements: A series of experiments", Information and Software Technology, 2009 (search summary: eight experiments; Fit tables improved comprehension of change requirements and correctness of the maintained code without significant extra effort). Melnik, Maurer, Chiasson, "Executable Acceptance Tests for Communicating Business Requirements: Customer Perspective", AGILE 2006. These are measurements of requester effort for change requests, mostly with students. They weaken the survey's "Thin ... the only measurements of requester effort found" and the README's "Unknown" on cost, without filling the repository-scale gap.
5. **An existing survey of the area.** Liang et al., "Test-Driven Approaches to Software Engineering with Large Language Models: A Survey of Phases, Tasks, and Agent Skills", arXiv:2609.12012 (abstract read): 87 records, organised by "what decision a test changes"; concludes that test availability, test validity, feedback use and evaluation independence are separate properties. The lab should read this before formulating; it may already contain part of the map.
6. **Selecting among candidates with generated tests, no human.** Chen et al., CodeT, arXiv:2207.10397 (ICLR 2023), the root of the "generated tests as filter" result that SWT-Bench extends. Ranking is parked in the programme, so this is context, not a change of conclusion.
7. Enoiu, "(Over)Reliance on Test Agents in AI-Assisted Software Testing", arXiv:2607.17927 (abstract read): a position paper with no data, arguing that generated tests get accepted as evidence without scrutiny. Supports item 1 as a concern only.

Items 1 and 2 would change the conclusion: the "requester confirms" route has unfavourable human evidence as well as favourable, and the survey reports only the favourable.

Nothing found contradicts the "Not found" claim about a map of requests by checkability.

## Standing of the "Current best answer" bullets

1. **"The literature shows that passing tests is not the same as being right, in both directions, even with developer-written tests."** **Stands**, as a qualitative claim, within the scope the bullet already states (one benchmark family, Python libraries, agent patches). It does not carry the "one in five" magnitude used in the README's "Where the field is" and in `survey.md` point 1: that should read as a range of roughly 8% to 20% on SWE-bench Verified depending on method, and should not be worded as a property of developer-written tests in general. The false-reject direction rests on two OpenAI blog posts, with METR's 1 in 27 as a small contrary data point; "shows" is earned for existence, not for a rate.
2. **"It suggests the cheapest trustworthy route for a new behaviour is a model-drafted failing check that the requester confirms."** **Does not stand as worded.** "Cheapest", "trustworthy" and "for a new behaviour" each lack a source. The evidence is consistent with this being a candidate worth testing. Replace with a statement that no route has measured error rates or requester cost at repository scale, that requester confirmation has one small favourable study and older unfavourable ones, and that it is a hypothesis for the benchmark.
3. **"Unknown: what share of real requests each route covers, and what any route costs a requester on repository-sized work."** **Stands.** Qualify the second half: pre-LLM acceptance-test experiments measured requester-side effort on small systems, so the gap is at repository scale and with generated checks, not total.

Line 2 inference in `survey.md` (not a README bullet): **weaken.** "Success only coarsely" stands and should be restated in tasks, not runs. "Steps and tokens well" is the lab's own inference with no cited support.
